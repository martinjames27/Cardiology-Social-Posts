## LINKEDIN POST

**We know semaglutide cuts major cardiovascular events by 20%. What we still don't fully understand is *why*.**

That's the central finding from a major mediation analysis of the SELECT trial, published this week in the European Heart Journal.

---

**Quick context on SELECT:**
17,604 patients. Established cardiovascular disease (prior MI, stroke, or symptomatic PAD). Obesity (BMI ≥27 kg/m²). No diabetes. Semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly versus placebo.

Result: 20% relative reduction in 3-point MACE (CV death, non-fatal MI, non-fatal stroke).

The natural assumption has always been: the heart protection comes from weight loss.

This week's analysis directly tests that assumption — and the results should make us think harder.

---

**What the mediation analysis found:**

Researchers quantified how much of the MACE reduction is explained by changes in eight measured cardiovascular risk factors: body weight, waist circumference, hsCRP, HbA1c, blood pressure, lipids, eGFR, and albumin-creatinine ratio.

Point estimates for individual mediators:

| Mediator | Estimated % of MACE Benefit Mediated |
|---|---|
| Waist circumference | ~64% |
| hsCRP (inflammation) | ~42% |
| HbA1c | ~29% |
| Body weight | ~20% |

But here's the critical nuance: these mediators overlap substantially, and together they still do not fully account for the observed cardiovascular benefit. A meaningful proportion of the MACE reduction remains unexplained — pointing toward mechanisms that our conventional risk factor framework simply doesn't capture.

The drug is doing something we can't yet measure.

---

**Why this matters clinically:**

For years, the argument in cardiovascular pharmacology was that GLP-1 benefits are downstream of weight loss — less adipose tissue, less inflammation, better metabolic control. This analysis complicates that story significantly.

Central adiposity (waist circumference) is the strongest individual mediator — not body weight on its own. That's an important distinction. It suggests that fat distribution — particularly visceral and potentially epicardial fat — matters more than total weight. For patients in high-risk groups with disproportionate visceral adiposity, including many South Asian patients who carry excess cardiometabolic risk even at lower BMI thresholds, this finding has real implications.

The leading hypotheses for the residual (unexplained) benefit include:
- Direct myocardial and anti-atherosclerotic effects
- Autonomic nervous system modulation
- Epicardial fat reduction
- Endothelial function improvement
- Plaque stabilization mechanisms not captured by traditional biomarkers

---

**The harder question:**

This isn't just about mechanism — it has consequences for how we counsel patients and payers.

When someone asks: "Isn't this just a weight loss drug?" — the evidence says: no. The cardiovascular protection is at least partially independent of the pounds lost on the scale.

For the cardiologist: this argues for thinking of semaglutide as a *cardiovascular intervention* that also causes weight loss — not the reverse.

For healthcare systems: it strengthens the case for broad coverage in high-risk CVD patients, regardless of how much weight they lose.

For those of us in preventive cardiology, this also reshapes how we think about "treating risk factors" versus "treating residual risk." The drug may be addressing mechanisms that sit upstream of the biomarkers we currently monitor.

---

**The bigger picture:**

As GLP-1 receptor agonists move deeper into cardiovascular medicine — and as tirzepatide accumulates its own trial evidence — the question of *mechanism* matters enormously. It shapes which patients benefit most, why some don't, and how to sequence these agents alongside statins, SGLT2 inhibitors, and PCSK9 inhibitors.

The SELECT mediation analysis is a rare study that delivers a landmark answer while simultaneously raising a more profound question.

We know it works. We still don't know exactly how.
