## LINKEDIN POST

**In multivessel STEMI, we treated half as many vessels — and patients did better. That's the finding from AIR-STEMI, just published in the New England Journal of Medicine.**

This counterintuitive result deserves serious attention from every cardiologist, interventionalist, fellow, and system leader thinking about how we make revascularization decisions in the acute setting.

---

**The clinical question has always been: when a patient presents with STEMI and additional non-culprit lesions, which ones — if any — do we treat?**

The standard answer has been guided by visual angiography: look at the lesion, estimate the degree of stenosis, decide. But visual estimation has well-known limitations. Stenosis severity by eye often doesn't predict whether a lesion is truly flow-limiting.

The AIR-STEMI trial tested a better approach: angiography-derived fractional flow reserve (FFR) — functional coronary angiography — to determine physiology from the same angiographic images already captured during the procedure. No pressure wire. No adenosine. Just better interpretation of the data we already have.

---

**What the trial found (1,823 patients, 21 centers, median follow-up 17.9 months):**

| Outcome | Physiology-Guided | Anatomy-Guided |
|---|---|---|
| Primary MACE (death/MI/stroke/revascularization) | **8.9%** | 13.7% |
| Hazard Ratio | **0.62** (95% CI 0.47–0.83) | — |
| p-value | **<0.001** | — |
| Non-culprit vessels stented | **51.9%** | 94.9% |
| Safety composite (AKI + major bleeding) | **4.6%** | 7.1% |

Half the stenting. Significantly better outcomes. Fewer complications.

---

**Why this matters beyond the headline number:**

**1. Less is more — when less is guided by data.**
This is not a trial saying complete revascularization is wrong. It is saying that anatomy-guided "complete" revascularization is frequently treating lesions that don't need treatment. Physiology identifies the right lesions. That distinction matters enormously for patient outcomes, procedural risk, and resource use.

**2. The technology is already here — and accessible.**
Angiography-derived FFR software derives physiological estimates directly from conventional coronary angiograms. No additional catheter equipment, no pharmacological stress, no extended procedure time. The barrier to implementing this at the point of care is lower than many assume.

**3. Fewer contrast-associated complications.**
Lower contrast volume translated to a halved rate of contrast-associated acute kidney injury and major bleeding in the functional group. As our STEMI patients increasingly present older with comorbid CKD, this is a meaningful safety dividend.

**4. A precision medicine moment in the cath lab.**
We talk often about precision medicine in genomics, in imaging, in risk stratification. AIR-STEMI shows precision decision-making can also transform what happens in the cath lab on the day of the acute event.

**5. The equity and generalizability question.**
The trial enrolled patients in Italy and Pakistan — an international cohort. How these findings extend to South Asian patients in the US and globally, who carry a disproportionate burden of premature multivessel CAD at younger ages, is a question worth studying directly. The physiology-guided approach could be particularly powerful in populations where aggressive revascularization carries higher procedural risk.

---

**The broader arc here is one I find compelling.**

Across cardiology, we are moving — consistently and deliberately — from anatomy-based to physiology-based and function-based decision-making. From FFR and iFR guiding elective PCI. To global longitudinal strain guiding oncologic decisions. To CCTA-derived plaque analysis redefining risk in asymptomatic individuals. To angiography-derived physiology now changing acute STEMI management.

The signal is clear: understanding *function*, not just *structure*, leads to better decisions and better outcomes.

AIR-STEMI is the latest — and perhaps most striking — proof point.
