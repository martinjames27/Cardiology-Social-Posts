## BEST ANGLE
Two landmark trials — Lp(a)HORIZON (pelacarsen) and ZEUS (ziltivekimab) — both hit their biological targets but missed their clinical endpoints, landing the same sobering lesson: biomarker reduction does not automatically equal cardiovascular event reduction. For Lp(a) specifically, the stakes are high — Mendelian randomization data have long suggested Lp(a) is causal for ASCVD, and next-generation Lp(a)-lowering drugs (olpasiran, muvalaplin, zerlasiran) are still in development. The field does not have its final answer yet, but Lp(a)HORIZON has fundamentally changed the question.

## LINKEDIN POST

**The most-anticipated preventive cardiology trial of the decade just reported its topline result. Lipoprotein(a) levels came down. Cardiovascular events did not.**

This is the Lp(a)HORIZON story. And it deserves a careful read before AHA 2026 next week, when the full data drop in Chicago.

---

**What the trial tested**

Lp(a)HORIZON randomized 8,323 patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (prior MI, ischemic stroke, or symptomatic PAD) and elevated Lp(a) ≥70 mg/dL to monthly subcutaneous pelacarsen versus placebo — on top of optimized standard of care. Pelacarsen is an antisense oligonucleotide (developed by Ionis, licensed to Novartis) that suppresses apolipoprotein(a) production in the liver.

The biological case was airtight. Mendelian randomization studies — which use genetic variants as proxies for lifelong exposure — show that Lp(a) is causally and specifically associated with ASCVD risk. Unlike LDL, which has multiple environmental modifiers, Lp(a) is ~80–90% genetically determined. If you lower it substantially, the theory says, events should follow.

Pelacarsen lowered Lp(a). Substantially. That much worked.

The MACE rate did not differ between groups.

---

**The numbers we have — and don't have**

As of today, we have topline data only. Novartis reported on September 4, 2026:
- Pelacarsen met its pharmacological goal — Lp(a) levels fell
- The primary composite endpoint (MACE: cardiovascular death, MI, stroke, urgent coronary revascularization) was not significantly reduced versus placebo
- The safety profile was acceptable

Full data — including hazard ratios, confidence intervals, subgroup analyses (including the pre-specified Lp(a) ≥90 mg/dL cohort), and event rates — will be presented at AHA 2026, November 6–9 in Chicago. That presentation will tell us much more about whether any population benefited.

---

**This is not the end of the Lp(a) story**

Here's what I think matters most for how we interpret this:

**1. Pelacarsen is not olpasiran or muvalaplin.**
Pelacarsen is an antisense oligonucleotide. Olpasiran (Amgen) is a small interfering RNA. These platforms have different pharmacological profiles, degrees of Lp(a) suppression, and potentially different off-target effects. The OCEAN(a)-DOSE trial showed olpasiran reduced Lp(a) by >95% in some patients. Its cardiovascular outcomes trial (OCEAN(a) Outcomes) is ongoing. The result with one drug does not indict the mechanism.

**2. Patient selection may matter more than we thought.**
Both Lp(a)HORIZON and ZEUS enrolled patients with existing disease, on maximally optimized therapy. If residual events on aggressive statin + PCSK9 inhibitor background therapy are driven by very different mechanisms than initial events, Lp(a) lowering may benefit a different population — perhaps true primary prevention patients, or those with very high Lp(a) (>150 mg/dL).

**3. Mendelian randomization may have told us the wrong question.**
MR estimates lifetime exposure effects — decades of elevated Lp(a) from birth. An interventional trial intervening in middle-aged patients with established disease for 3–5 years may not replicate that biological window. This is the same tension we saw with lipoprotein(b), fibrinogen, and others.

**4. The ZEUS parallel is instructive.**
Ziltivekimab, an IL-6 ligand trap from Novo Nordisk, also missed its primary MACE endpoint (HR 0.99; 95% CI 0.88–1.11) in 6,300+ patients with ASCVD, CKD, and elevated hsCRP ≥2 mg/L — despite confirmed hsCRP and IL-6 suppression. Two trials, two biomarkers, two null MACE results. The "treat the residual biomarker" strategy is under genuine scrutiny.

---

**What this means for clinical practice — right now**

| Question | Where we stand |
|---|---|
| Should I still test Lp(a) in patients? | **Yes.** Lp(a) remains a validated risk-enhancing factor in ACC/AHA guidelines. Testing guides risk discussions and intensity of proven therapies. |
| Should I tell patients we can treat their Lp(a)? | **Not yet with proven outcomes benefit.** Niacin trials also failed. PCSK9 inhibitors modestly lower Lp(a) (~25%) but CV benefit is attributable to LDL reduction. |
| Is Lp(a) no longer a causal risk factor? | **Uncertain.** One trial, one agent, one patient population. MR evidence remains. The causal hypothesis isn't disproven, but the interventional chain is broken for now. |
| What about high-Lp(a) South Asian patients? | **Clinical judgment + aggressive established risk reduction.** South Asians have higher Lp(a) burden and premature CAD. Until next-gen agents have outcome data, statin + lifestyle + PCSK9 (where indicated) remain primary. |
