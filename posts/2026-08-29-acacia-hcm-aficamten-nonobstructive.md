## LINKEDIN POST (Main — 400-900 words)

One of the most frustrating conversations in cardiology has been telling a patient with non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: *"There's nothing available that changes the underlying problem."*

That may be about to change.

At ESC Congress 2026 in Munich — happening this week — the **ACACIA-HCM trial** results were presented in a Hot Line session and simultaneously published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*. This is the kind of data that reshapes a disease category.

**The population that has been waiting**

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) affects approximately 1 in 500 people — one of the most common inherited cardiovascular conditions. Roughly 30-40% of HCM patients do not have significant left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction. These *non-obstructive* HCM patients have been largely left without disease-modifying treatment options.

Mavacamten (Camzyos), the approved cardiac myosin inhibitor, was studied and indicated for *obstructive* HCM — patients with LVOT gradients. For the non-obstructive group: beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, and supportive care. Nothing that acts at the disease mechanism.

**What ACACIA-HCM found**

ACACIA-HCM was a Phase 3, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of **aficamten** — a selective cardiac myosin inhibitor — in patients with symptomatic non-obstructive HCM. After 36 weeks:

| Endpoint | Aficamten | Placebo | P-value |
|---|---|---|---|
| KCCQ-CSS improvement | +11.4 points | +8.4 points | P = .021 |
| Peak VO₂ (pVO₂) | +0.64 mL/kg/min | −0.03 mL/kg/min | P = .003 |
| NYHA functional class | Significantly improved | — | Significant |
| NT-proBNP | Significantly reduced | — | Significant |

Both dual primary endpoints were met. Critically, the peak VO₂ result tells a particularly clear story: patients on aficamten gained meaningful exercise capacity, while those on placebo declined. This is not a surrogate — it reflects real functional trajectory.

Safety was favorable: only 2 participants in the aficamten arm experienced serious adverse events related to LVEF reduction below 50%, consistent with the drug's known mechanism of action.

**Why this matters for clinical practice**

This is the first positive Phase 3 trial in symptomatic non-obstructive HCM. If aficamten receives regulatory approval for this indication, it would be the first disease-modifying therapy available to this population.

Consider also the class-level validation: aficamten now has Phase 3 efficacy data in *both* obstructive HCM (MAPLE-HCM, ESC 2025) and non-obstructive HCM (ACACIA-HCM, ESC 2026). The cardiac myosin inhibitor class is no longer a single-indication story.

**The questions worth asking**

Data this significant deserves scrutiny:

- The KCCQ-CSS difference was 3 absolute points. In a condition with high placebo response, how will clinicians and payers weigh this against drug cost?
- Non-obstructive HCM is phenotypically heterogeneous. Which patients — by genetics, hemodynamics, or degree of fibrosis — benefit most?
- Will symptom benefit translate to hard outcomes: arrhythmia reduction, HF hospitalization, mortality?
- **Access and equity**: Myosin inhibitors carry significant cost. Who will realistically be able to access this therapy if approved? This is a conversation we need to have before, not after, FDA submission.

These aren't reasons for skepticism — they are the questions rigorous post-approval study must answer. The data earns this drug a serious regulatory review.

Munich this week is presenting results from 59 pivotal trials in 12 Hot Line sessions. The pace of cardiovascular innovation is extraordinary: myosin inhibitors, PCSK9/RNA-based lipid therapies, GLP-1 agonists in heart failure, AI-enabled coronary imaging. We are in a remarkable period.
