## LINKEDIN POST

**You ordered the Lp(a). It came back elevated. The CAC is zero. Now what?**

This is one of the most common — and genuinely difficult — counseling moments in preventive cardiology right now. The 2026 ACC/AHA Dyslipidemia Guideline made universal Lp(a) testing a standard recommendation for all adults. That's the right move. But it also created a generation of patients and clinicians staring at an elevated number without a clear playbook.

A new multicohort study just published in JACC (2026;87:20, pp. 2864–2872) brings important clarity.

**What the study found:**

Over a 15-year follow-up, both elevated Lp(a) (>50 mg/dL) and a CAC score >0 were **independent** predictors of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and coronary heart disease — even after accounting for each other and traditional risk factors.

| Risk Factor | Hazard Ratio for ASCVD |
|---|---|
| Lp(a) >50 mg/dL | 1.24 |
| CAC >0 | 2.44 |
| Both elevated | Additive, independent |

That's a meaningful signal. But here's the nuance that should shape your counseling:

Among patients with **CAC = 0**, elevated Lp(a) still carried a greater **relative** risk of ASCVD and CHD across all strata. However, the **absolute** risk of events remained low — even when Lp(a) was elevated. This distinction matters enormously when you're sitting across from a 45-year-old patient deciding whether to start a PCSK9 inhibitor or escalate therapy.

**What this means clinically:**

The old reflex — "CAC of zero means you can reassure the patient and step back" — needs updating when Lp(a) is elevated. These patients aren't zero-risk. They may be appropriate for closer follow-up, earlier repeat CAC imaging, and more aggressive lifestyle and LDL-lowering in the interim.

At the same time, the persistently low absolute risk in the CAC-zero/elevated-Lp(a) group should temper reflexive escalation to expensive or invasive therapies without a fuller risk conversation.

**Why South Asians deserve special attention here:**

This isn't just a general preventive cardiology story. For those of us who care for South Asian patients, this data lands differently.

South Asian individuals have:
- Higher median Lp(a) levels (~17 mg/dL vs ~13 mg/dL in White Americans)
- ASCVD rates approximately 2.5× higher than non-Hispanic White populations
- More premature coronary artery disease — often presenting a decade earlier
- Frequent underestimation of true risk by traditional pooled cohort equations

The 2026 guideline explicitly names South Asian ancestry as a high-risk category warranting more aggressive lipid management. Combining that recommendation with this new Lp(a)+CAC data gives us a more precise framework for the patients most likely to benefit.

**The practical takeaway:**

1. **Test Lp(a) once in every adult** — the guideline is clear, the test is underutilized
2. **If Lp(a) >50 mg/dL, add CAC** — this combination sharpens the risk conversation and guides intensity of therapy
3. **CAC = 0 is reassuring but not exonerating** — especially in younger patients, South Asians, and those with other risk-enhancing features
4. **Elevated Lp(a) should prompt aggressive management of all modifiable risk factors** — LDL, blood pressure, lifestyle — even in the absence of approved Lp(a)-specific therapy

We are in a transitional moment. Lp(a)-targeted therapies (RNA-based and others) are in late-stage development. The infrastructure we build now — identifying who has elevated Lp(a), what their CAC score tells us, and how to stratify them — is the foundation for deploying those therapies well when they arrive.

**The question I'm sitting with:**

When you find elevated Lp(a) and a CAC of zero, how are you framing the conversation with your patient? Are you reassuring them, intensifying, or taking a "watchful waiting + aggressive risk factor control" approach?

I'd love to hear how colleagues across preventive cardiology, primary care, and lipidology are handling this — the clinical decisions being made right now will define best practice for years.
