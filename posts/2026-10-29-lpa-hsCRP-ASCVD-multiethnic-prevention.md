## LINKEDIN POST

**We keep treating South Asian patients as high risk — but we keep measuring them the same way as everyone else.**

The HELIUS cohort study, published in JACC, offers a compelling reason to change that.

In 15,676 participants from Amsterdam — including large cohorts of South Asian Surinamese, African Surinamese, and European Dutch origin — researchers tracked incident ASCVD over a median of 8.9 years using nationwide registries. They then asked a deceptively simple question: does combining LDL-C, Lp(a), and hsCRP add more than any of these markers alone?

The answer: yes. Substantially.

**What they found:**

| Biomarker | Top vs. Bottom Quintile HR |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| LDL-C     | 1.39 |
| Lp(a)     | 1.86 |
| hsCRP     | 1.51 |

When all three were elevated? **2.44-fold increased ASCVD risk** (95% CI: 1.46–4.09).

Adding Lp(a) and hsCRP to an ASCVD prediction model improved discrimination (ΔAUC 0.006, p=0.030) and reclassification (NRI 0.165) — modest but statistically meaningful improvements that could move patients from "borderline" to "high risk" in a clinical conversation.

**The finding that should stop every preventive cardiologist:**

While the relative risk gradients were consistent across all three ethnic groups, **absolute ASCVD risk was highest among South Asian Surinamese participants** — even after accounting for traditional risk factors.

We already know South Asians carry disproportionate premature coronary artery disease burden globally. We know that LDL-C alone systematically underestimates their risk. This study adds granular data: Lp(a) — elevated in approximately 25% of South Asians worldwide — and systemic inflammation together compound what LDL-C misses.

**Why this matters now:**

The 2026 ACC/AHA Dyslipidemia Guideline has finally moved universal Lp(a) screening into mainstream recommendations. We now have a clear biological rationale and prospective cohort data showing *who benefits most* from adding it — and South Asians are near the top of that list.

The clinical workflow is straightforward:
- One-time Lp(a) in all patients (especially South Asian ancestry)
- hsCRP when borderline risk and statin initiation decision is uncertain
- Use all three in context, not in isolation

The authors are right to caution against retrofitting a new risk score. But the signal here isn't subtle — it's a 2.44-fold gradient. For patients who "don't qualify" for statins by traditional LDL-C thresholds but carry elevated Lp(a) and elevated hsCRP? This study argues we should be having a different conversation with them.

**The bottom line:** Risk assessment in diverse populations can't be an afterthought. When South Asians present with premature CAD, we ask why we missed it. Studies like HELIUS show us how to miss it less.

What's your current approach to Lp(a) screening in South Asian patients — routine, selective, or still waiting for more evidence? Curious where others land.
