## LINKEDIN POST

When a South Asian patient with a "normal" cardiac workup has a heart attack at 48, something in our risk assessment framework has failed.

We've known for decades that South Asians develop coronary artery disease younger, more severely, and often with less warning. What we've lacked is precise, imaging-based data on exactly what's happening in their coronary arteries before symptoms appear.

New research from the DILWALE CTA study fills that gap—and the numbers should reshape how we approach this population.

---

**What the study found**

Using AI-enabled quantitative coronary plaque analysis (AI-QCPA), researchers evaluated 341 South Asian adults (median age 54, 31% women) within the Baylor Scott & White Health system. The findings:

| Finding | Result |
|---|---|
| Patients with any coronary plaque | 63% |
| Aged 40–54 with plaque | ~50% |
| Plaque that was noncalcified (NCP) | 86% |
| NCP prevalence pattern | Dominant across all age groups |
| Calcified plaque trend | Increased with age |
| Sex difference | Men higher overall; women sharp catch-up after 65 |

This is not incidental finding territory. Noncalcified plaque—particularly low-attenuation plaque—is the subtype most associated with acute coronary syndromes. Finding it as the dominant plaque type in nearly two-thirds of South Asian adults, including half of those in their 40s, is clinically significant.

---

**Why this matters for risk stratification**

Standard risk calculators were not built with South Asians in mind. The Pooled Cohort Equations consistently underestimate cardiovascular risk in this population. The new PREVENT equations—now the standard in the 2026 ACC/AHA Dyslipidemia Guideline—improved calibration and removed race as a variable, but they still don't fully capture the unique risk profile of South Asians, who tend toward earlier, more diffuse atherosclerosis, often with normal BMI and only modestly elevated LDL.

A coronary artery calcium (CAC) score of zero in a South Asian patient in their 40s can be falsely reassuring if the dominant disease pattern is noncalcified plaque—which a CAC score, by definition, doesn't capture.

CCTA with AI-assisted plaque analysis changes that.

The 2026 Dyslipidemia Guideline specifically names South Asian ancestry as a risk-enhancing factor and identifies CCTA as a tool to consider in patients where plaque burden may exceed what laboratory markers suggest. The DILWALE data provides exactly the empirical foundation that recommendation needs.

---

**The AI component**

AI-QCPA enables standardized, reproducible, quantitative analysis of plaque volume, composition, and morphology at scale—something manual interpretation alone cannot reliably achieve across large, heterogeneous populations. The DILWALE study demonstrates its feasibility in a real-world, EHR-based South Asian cohort.

This is AI doing what it does best: making precision quantification accessible at the point of care, not just in research centers.

---

**What should change in practice**

1. **Lower the threshold for CCTA** in South Asian patients, particularly those in their 40s with even modest risk factor burden
2. **Don't stop at CAC scoring**—noncalcified plaque requires soft tissue characterization that CAC cannot provide
3. **Include AI-QCPA where available** to quantify plaque burden and morphology beyond visual assessment
4. **South Asian ancestry is not just a risk modifier**—it should function as a clinically active prompt to screen earlier and more deeply
5. **Revisit patients with reassuring labs**—a normal LDL in a South Asian patient with family history of premature CAD is not the same as low risk

There is a well-documented treatment gap in this population. High-risk patients are under-recognized, undertreated, and underrepresented in the trials that generate the evidence we rely on. Studies like DILWALE CTA are beginning to correct the evidentiary record.

---

**Looking ahead**

This is a retrospective registry from a single health system with a relatively small cohort. Prospective, multicenter studies with defined endpoints and longitudinal follow-up are needed. But the direction is clear: South Asians carry a premature atherosclerotic burden that standard tools miss, AI-enabled CCTA offers a window into that burden, and the evidence base for acting on what we find is growing.

For a population that now represents 5–7% of the US—and that disproportionately presents with heart attacks before 50—that window matters.

## OTHER STRONG FINDS TODAY

**1. 2026 Dyslipidemia Guideline — JAMA Population Impact Analyses** *(JAMA, July 21 & 28, 2026)*
Two companion JAMA analyses show the new ACC/AHA guideline adds 21.5 million Americans to statin eligibility (primarily younger, lower-risk adults aged 30–79), replaces the Pooled Cohort Equations with PREVENT, and introduces 30-year ASCVD risk assessment as a new primary prevention pathway. Strongest alternate post topic this week — broad practice impact, high engagement potential.

**2. GBD 2023 — Modifiable Risk Factors and IHD Mortality in US States** *(JAMA Cardiology, July 15, 2026)*
88.7% of ischemic heart disease deaths in 2023 were attributable to modifiable risk factors. Top three: high systolic blood pressure (47%), dietary risks (39%), high LDL-C (29%). BMI and fasting plasma glucose showed the largest increases as contributors since 1990 (+55% and +39%, respectively). ~473,000 IHD deaths analyzed across all US states. Powerful preventive cardiology frame with geographic equity angle.

**3. IL-6 Predicts 9 Cardiovascular Outcomes Across 320,000+ Participants** *(JACC, May 2026; author interview July 21, 2026)*
Data from 25 prospective cohort studies (the Cross Cohort Collaboration) confirm that elevated serum interleukin-6 independently predicts MI, stroke, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, cardiovascular mortality, and all-cause mortality. Raises the question of whether IL-6 should enter routine CV risk stratification alongside hsCRP—and underscores the growing role of anti-inflammatory therapy (ziltivekimab, canakinumab) in preventive cardiology.
