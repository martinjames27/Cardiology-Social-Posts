## LINKEDIN POST

**What if the most dangerous place for a heart failure patient was the hospital?**

That's not a rhetorical provocation. It's increasingly what the data suggest — and the SUBCUT HF II trial, just published in JACC, puts numbers to it.

---

**The problem with our default:**

Heart failure is the leading cause of hospitalization in adults over 65. The average index admission stretches to 10–11 days. Thirty-day readmission rates hover around 25%. The U.S. spends over $30 billion annually on HF-related inpatient care.

We've optimized the wrong thing. We've gotten very good at managing patients *inside* hospitals. We haven't asked hard enough whether they need to be there.

---

**What SUBCUT HF II did:**

This multicenter, open-label randomized controlled trial enrolled 172 patients hospitalized with acute decompensated heart failure requiring IV loop diuretics — across 20 NHS hospitals in the UK.

Patients were randomized to:
- **Early supported discharge** with subcutaneous furosemide via a wearable pump (Lasix ONYU, FDA-approved October 2025), or
- **Standard inpatient care** with continued IV furosemide

The primary endpoint: **days alive and out of hospital at 30 days.**

---

**The results:**

| Outcome | Sub-Q Early Discharge | Standard Care | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Days alive & out of hospital (30d) | Higher | Lower | **+4.0 days** (95% CI 2.1–5.8; P<0.001) |
| Index hospital stay | **5.6 days** | 11.0 days | −5.4 days |
| Days alive & out of hospital (60d) | **53.9 days** | 49.7 days | +4.2 days |

Patients in the early-discharge arm went home nearly twice as fast. And they spent significantly more of the following month outside hospital walls — not readmitted, not in the ED, not waiting for a bed.

No device-related serious adverse events were reported.

---

**Why this matters beyond the p-value:**

This trial isn't just about a drug delivery device. It's a proof of concept for a fundamentally different care model — one that says: *if we can safely match the diuretic efficacy of IV therapy at home, we should.*

The implications ripple across:

**Clinical care:** Patients with HF who qualify can be decongested at home after initial stabilization. The wearable subcutaneous pump delivers furosemide continuously, matching or exceeding the bioavailability of IV therapy in fluid-overloaded patients.

**Patient experience:** Eleven days in a hospital bed, tethered to an IV pole, disrupts sleep, nutrition, mobility, and dignity. For older patients especially, prolonged hospitalization accelerates deconditioning. Four extra days at home is not a rounding error — it's meaningful life.

**Healthcare economics:** A 5.4-day reduction in index length of stay, at average HF hospitalization costs of $15,000–20,000, represents staggering potential savings per patient — let alone across the 1+ million annual HF hospitalizations in the U.S.

**Equity and access:** Hospital-at-home models have historically favored patients with stable home environments and reliable follow-up. This trial raises important equity questions: who benefits, who gets excluded, and how do we design programs that reach underserved populations?

**Operations:** For health systems, shorter stays improve throughput, reduce bottlenecks, and free capacity. For accountable care organizations and bundle payment programs, this is a direct lever on cost.

---

**What comes next:**

Lasix ONYU (subcutaneous furosemide) received FDA approval in October 2025 for edema in chronic heart failure. This trial provides the randomized evidence that early-discharge protocols built around it are not just feasible — they're better.

The question is no longer *can we?* It's: **Do our care teams, discharge infrastructure, remote monitoring capabilities, and payer models support doing this at scale?**

For most U.S. health systems: not yet. But the evidence has arrived ahead of the infrastructure. That gap is where the next decade of HF innovation will be built.
